To meet the need for a biodegradable polyester that withstands elevated temperatures without unduly sacrificing degradation kinetics, the core strategy is to decouple thermal stability from crystallinity and hydrophobicity. High heat resistance is typically achieved through stiff chains and high crystallinity, but crystallinity severely restricts water and enzyme access, while hydrophobic rigid structures slow hydrolysis. The following molecular design breaks this trade-off.

### Key Design Elements  
1. **Rigid, kinked diol for high Tg and amorphous morphology** – Isosorbide (1,4:3,6-dianhydro-D-glucitol) is a bio-based, V-shaped diol. Its fused tetrahydrofuran rings impart chain stiffness, drastically raising the glass transition temperature (Tg) compared to linear aliphatic diols. Because of its chirality and kinked geometry, isosorbide‑based polyesters are usually fully amorphous, eliminating dense crystalline domains that hinder degradation.  
2. **Mixed aliphatic diacids with hydrolysable units** – Succinic acid provides biodegradable ester linkages. A minor fraction of a sulfonated aromatic diacid (e.g., dimethyl 5‑sulfoisophthalate sodium salt, DSSIP) introduces pendant sulfonate groups that enhance hydrophilicity and water uptake, accelerating hydrolytic cleavage without compromising overall chain stiffness.  
3. **Flexible co‑diol (optional)** – A small amount of 1,4‑butanediol can be added to avoid excessive brittleness while preserving a high Tg window.  
4. **Labile anhydride linkages (optional speed‑control)** – Introducing reactive end‑capping or a few percent of succinic anhydride units creates more rapidly hydrolysing sites, providing an additional kinetic lever.

### Proposed Polymer Structure  
A random copolyester synthesized via two‑step melt polycondensation:  
> Poly[(isosorbide succinate)-co-(isosorbide 5‑sulfoisophthalate)-co-(butylene succinate)]

For example, a feed composition (molar ratios): isosorbide 0.8, 1,4‑butanediol 0.2, succinic acid 0.85, DSSIP 0.15. The actual incorporation can be tuned based on Tg and degradation targets.

### Why This Balances Heat Resistance and Degradation  
- **Thermal properties**: The rigid isosorbide units and the aromatic sulfoisophthalate raise Tg well above 80 °C (often 90–110 °C for similar systems), giving the material good dimensional stability up to boiling‑water temperatures. The amorphous nature avoids a sharp melting point, while the high Tg prevents softening below the desired service temperature.  
- **Degradation kinetics**:  
  - Complete amorphicity allows water to permeate the bulk uniformly, so hydrolysis is not limited to lamellar surfaces.  
  - The ionic sulfonate groups create hydrophilic nano‑channels, increasing water solubility and local acidity, which catalyses ester bond cleavage.  
  - The hydrolysable ester bonds in the succinate and sulfonated units remain sterically accessible because the kinked isosorbide prevents tight chain packing.  
  - Any additional anhydride linkages serve as “degradation triggers”, ensuring an initial mass‑loss rate comparable to or faster than conventional aliphatic polyesters.  
- **Biodegradability**: The final breakdown products (isosorbide, succinic acid, butanediol, sulfonated monomer) are small molecules that have been shown to be environmentally benign. The aliphatic ester backbone is the primary substrate for many microbial depolymerases.

### Experimental Verification (Outline)  
- **Synthesis**: Two‑stage polycondensation (esterification at 180–200 °C with Ti(OBu)₄ catalyst, then polycondensation at 220–240 °C under vacuum).  
- **Thermal analysis**: DSC confirms Tg > 85 °C and absence of melting peaks; TGA shows degradation onset near 300 °C.  
- **Hydrolytic degradation**: Samples immersed in phosphate buffer (pH 7.4, 37 °C) monitored for mass loss, molecular weight (GPC), and mechanical properties. Compare to PLLA or PBS benchmarks.  
- **Enzymatic degradation**: Using lipases or cutinases to assess real‑world compostability kinetics.

By relying on inherent amorphousness and controlled hydrophilicity, this design pushes the heat‑resistance envelope of biodegradable polyesters while retaining, and in some cases even enhancing, their hydrolytic and enzymatic decomposition rates.
