A good design is a **mostly aliphatic, lightly rigidified random copolyester**: enough rigid content to raise heat resistance, but not enough aromatic/rigid crystallinity to make the material persist too long.

## Proposed polymer

### Random copolyester composition

**Poly(butylene succinate-co-butylene furandicarboxylate-co-glycolate)**

Approximate repeat-unit composition:

- **75–85 mol% butylene succinate units**  
  Main biodegradable aliphatic polyester segment; comparable to PBS.
- **10–15 mol% butylene 2,5-furandicarboxylate units**  
  Rigid bio-based furan ring raises Tg, modulus, melt strength, and heat distortion temperature.
- **3–8 mol% glycolate units**  
  Adds hydrolytically labile, more hydrophilic ester sites to prevent degradation from becoming too slow.

A representative target:

\[
\text{P(BS}_{80}\text{-BF}_{15}\text{-G}_{5}\text{)}
\]

where BS = butylene succinate, BF = butylene furandicarboxylate, and G = glycolate.

## Why this design works

### 1. Improved heat resistance

PBS alone has decent biodegradability but limited heat resistance, with a melting point around 110–115 °C and relatively low stiffness at elevated temperature.

Adding **10–15 mol% FDCA-derived furan units** improves heat resistance because the furan ring:

- Increases backbone rigidity.
- Raises the glass transition temperature.
- Improves dimensional stability.
- Can increase crystallization effectiveness if kept at modest levels.
- Provides some of the thermal benefits of aromatic units without using large amounts of terephthalate.

The goal is not to make a fully semi-aromatic polyester like PBAT or PBF-rich materials, but to use a **small rigid fraction** to raise heat performance while maintaining biodegradability.

### 2. Degradation kinetics are not excessively suppressed

High aromatic or furan content can slow biodegradation because:

- Rigid units reduce chain mobility.
- Higher crystallinity reduces water and enzyme access.
- Aromatic-rich domains are less readily metabolized.

To avoid that, the furan content should be kept around **10–15 mol%**, preferably below 20 mol%.

The addition of **3–8 mol% glycolate units** helps compensate by:

- Increasing hydrolysis susceptibility.
- Disrupting overly perfect crystallites.
- Increasing water uptake slightly.
- Creating more accessible amorphous regions.

This gives a balanced structure: enough crystallinity and rigidity for heat resistance, but enough amorphous/hydrolysable content for degradation.

## Suggested formulation

| Component | Function | Suggested level |
|---|---:|---:|
| Succinic acid or dimethyl succinate | Main biodegradable aliphatic diacid | 75–85 mol% of acid units |
| 2,5-Furandicarboxylic acid or dimethyl FDCA | Rigid heat-resistant unit | 10–15 mol% of acid units |
| Glycolide or glycolic acid derivative | Fast-hydrolyzing degradation accelerator | 3–8 mol% of total repeat units |
| 1,4-Butanediol | Main diol | Stoichiometric with diacids |
| Biodegradable nucleating agent | Faster crystallization, better HDT | 0.2–1 wt% |

Possible nucleating agents:

- Cellulose nanocrystals
- Talc, if mineral filler is acceptable
- Calcium carbonate
- Biochar or lignin-derived particles, if well dispersed

## Processing strategy

To improve heat resistance without relying on excessive rigid monomer content:

1. **Use random copolycondensation**, not blocky furan-rich sequences.  
   Blocky rigid domains may raise heat resistance but suppress degradation more strongly.

2. **Target moderate molecular weight**, e.g.  
   \[
   M_n \approx 50{,}000–90{,}000 \text{ g/mol}
   \]  
   Too low gives poor mechanical/thermal properties; too high can slow degradation.

3. **Control crystallinity to around 25–40%.**  
   Very high crystallinity improves HDT but greatly slows biodegradation.

4. **Anneal after molding**, e.g. 80–95 °C for several minutes.  
   This increases heat distortion resistance through crystallization without needing more furan content.

## Expected property changes versus PBS

Compared with neat PBS, the designed copolyester should show:

- Higher Tg.
- Improved modulus at elevated temperature.
- Higher heat distortion temperature.
- Similar or modestly reduced biodegradation rate, depending on crystallinity.
- Better balance than a highly aromatic biodegradable polyester.

Qualitatively:

| Property | PBS | Proposed P(BS-BF-G) |
|---|---:|---:|
| Heat resistance | Moderate | Improved |
| Crystallization | Good | Moderate to good with nucleator |
| Hydrolytic degradability | Good | Slightly slower, compensated by glycolate |
| Compost biodegradation | Good | Should remain acceptable if BF ≤15–20 mol% |
| Brittleness | Moderate | Can be controlled with glycolate/adipate content |

## Alternative variant

If more flexibility is needed, replace part of the glycolate with adipate:

\[
\text{P(BS}_{75}\text{-BA}_{10}\text{-BF}_{15}\text{)}
\]

where BA = butylene adipate.

This gives better toughness and biodegradability but slightly lower heat resistance than the glycolate-containing version.

## Final recommended design

A strong balanced candidate is:

\[
\boxed{\text{Poly(butylene succinate-co-15 mol% butylene furandicarboxylate-co-5 mol% glycolate)}}
\]

with:

- Random sequence distribution.
- 0.5 wt% biodegradable nucleating agent.
- Moderate molecular weight.
- Controlled annealing to achieve 25–40% crystallinity.

This design improves heat resistance through limited rigid furan incorporation and crystallization control, while maintaining degradation by keeping the rigid content low and adding a small amount of hydrolytically labile glycolate units.
